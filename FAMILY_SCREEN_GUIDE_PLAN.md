# Life at Hand: family screen guide product plan

## The decision

Build the family screen problem as the clearest first on-ramp into Life at Hand.

This does not turn the whole brand into a parenting company. It creates one sharp door into the larger work of attention, relationships, family life, and human connection.

The public promise is:

> **Fewer screen fights. More real life.**  
> A practical seven-day family plan for healthier limits, calmer transitions, and better things to do when the screens go off.

Do not sell a “digital detox,” a perfect screen-free childhood, a medical outcome, or parental guilt. Sell a usable plan for the next seven days.

## Why this is the best on-ramp

- Parents already recognize the problem. The marketing does not have to teach them that divided attention exists.
- The pain is concrete: arguments when a device goes off, bedtime drift, adults checking phones around children, worry about content, and not knowing what should replace the screen.
- The result is observable within a week. A family can tell whether transitions became calmer, a screen-free zone held, or one off-screen activity actually happened.
- It leads naturally into the existing adult attention work. Parents cannot credibly demand habits they refuse to examine in themselves.
- It creates useful public content that people actively search for and share with spouses, friends, schools, churches, pediatric practices, and parent groups.

## What makes this different

The internet already has screen-time charts, parental-control instructions, device blockers, and fear-based warnings. Life at Hand should solve the implementation gap.

The differentiator is:

1. The adult goes first.
2. The family changes one or two moments, not its entire life overnight.
3. The plan distinguishes communication, school, creativity, social connection, regulation, and entertainment use.
4. The family chooses what will replace the screen before removing it.
5. The plan adapts to age, development, temperament, neurodivergence, work schedules, and family structure.
6. A bad night is expected and repaired. It is not proof the plan failed.

## Offer structure

### Free: Family Screen Starter

One genuinely useful printable and a short companion page.

Contents:

- a two-minute adult screen self-check
- a prompt to choose one screen-free time and one screen-free place
- a short family-conversation script
- an off-screen replacement menu organized by energy and parent availability
- a seven-day experiment box
- a “what we noticed” review

The printable should use a white background, black or deep-green text, one small color accent, and no full-bleed art. The webpage can carry the richer color and imagery.

Primary call to action:

> Send me the free Family Screen Starter

The email follow-up should deliver the guide immediately, ask one useful question on day three, and invite the parent to the paid beta only after they have had a chance to use it.

### Paid beta: The Family Screen Guide

Recommended founding price: **$39 one time**.

Delivery:

- seven short browser-based lessons
- approximately five to eight minutes per lesson
- one printable and editable parent workbook
- age-specific quick-reference pages
- family agreement template
- visual transition cards that can be printed without heavy ink
- Apple, Android, game-console, and streaming-device setting links that are dated and maintained separately from the core lessons
- optional weekly application session or parent group after the self-guided beta proves useful

Avoid permanent “lifetime updates” language. Device settings change. Promise access to the purchased edition and state how long technical-setting updates are included.

## Seven-day learning sequence

### Day 1: Start with the adults

Notice when, where, and why the adults reach for screens around the family. Choose one behavior the adults will change first.

### Day 2: Learn what the screen is doing

Use the AAP 5 C's as a plain-language check: child, content, calm, crowding out, and communication. A screen may be helping with communication, learning, focused interest, regulation, connection, or needed parental breathing room. Name the function before changing the habit.

### Day 3: Choose the first boundary

Pick one time and one place. Examples include meals, the first twenty minutes after arriving home, the car ride to school, bedrooms overnight, or the hour before bed. Do not begin with a long list of rules.

### Day 4: Make the next thing easier

Build a replacement menu before the screen turns off. Organize choices by:

- five minutes or thirty minutes
- independent or together
- quiet or active
- indoors or outdoors
- low parent energy or high parent energy
- sensory calming or sensory seeking

### Day 5: Make transitions predictable

Use advance notice, visual cues, a clear stopping point, a preview of what comes next, and a calm script. Include a plan for when a child or parent cannot make the transition well.

### Day 6: Set the devices to support the agreement

Use built-in limits, downtime, content settings, notification changes, and charging locations. Technology supports the family agreement; it does not replace conversation or adult modeling.

### Day 7: Keep what worked

Review one week without grading the family. Keep one useful boundary, change one unrealistic part, and decide when the plan will be reviewed again.

## Age-specific paths

### Ages 2–5

Emphasize co-use, routines, sleep, language, movement, play, and predictable transitions. The plan is mainly for the adults.

### Ages 6–10

Use a simple shared agreement, visible schedules, content awareness, play alternatives, and consistent times and places. Give children meaningful but bounded choices.

### Ages 11–14

Address gaming, group chats, social pressure, school devices, privacy, first phones, and why the adults also have rules. Include conversation prompts, not surveillance theater.

### Ages 15–18

Treat the teenager as a participant with growing judgment and real social needs. Discuss sleep, driving, school, privacy, harmful content, algorithms, and mutually understood safety boundaries. Do not market total parental control as the goal.

## Neurodivergent-family adaptation

This is required, but it must be reviewed by qualified professionals before sale.

Principles:

- do not treat autism or ADHD as a screen-time problem
- do not remove communication, organization, or regulation support without a replacement plan
- use predictable routines, visual transitions, and concrete expectations
- account for sensory needs, focused interests, social energy, motor needs, and transition difficulty
- separate required, creative, communicative, regulating, social, and entertainment use
- offer scripts and replacement menus that can be individualized with the child's care or school team
- never imply that one family's experience proves what another neurodivergent child needs

### Permanent child-privacy rule

The founders' child is not part of the brand. Never use the child's name, diagnosis, image, words, behavior, story, or recognizable details in the product, public copy, art, interviews, testimonials, or marketing. The parents may use what they have privately learned to ask better questions and build more compassionate tools. The published teaching must be generalized, source-grounded, de-identified, and professionally reviewed.

## Optional faith layer

The general guide should be fully usable without a religious commitment. Add a clearly labeled optional Christian reflection only after it has been lived and reviewed.

Possible themes:

- attention as a form of love
- rest and Sabbath instead of constant stimulation
- gratitude for ordinary life
- the table as a place of presence and hospitality
- serving a neighbor or family member with the time made available
- what the family wants technology to serve, rather than what it wants technology to forbid

The founders should speak as parents and learners, not as clergy or clinicians. A trusted pastor or qualified Christian family practitioner should review Scripture use and group material. Faith should deepen the practice, not amplify fear or shame.

## Art and design brief

The family offer should feel like the same authored world as the main site.

Use:

- current families in believable homes, clothes, vehicles, schools, parks, and community spaces
- mid-century print texture and composition without period costumes
- emotional moments with a clear human truth: the missed ball game, the phone set face-down, a difficult transition handled gently, a child absorbed in making something, a teenager talking in the car
- different ages, family structures, races, abilities, and kinds of homes without turning inclusion into a checklist collage
- physically plausible hands, furniture, sports gear, bicycles, cars, devices, and body positions
- strong crops that preserve faces, eye lines, relationships, and the action of the scene

Avoid:

- fake timers, clocks, phone interfaces, text, and generated numbers
- a parent looming over a “problem child”
- a glowing device as the only visual metaphor
- perfect-family advertising smiles
- dark crisis imagery
- nostalgic families that read as the 1950s or 1960s
- full-color backgrounds on printables

## Beta and proof plan

Recruit ten to fifteen families across at least three age bands. Include several family structures and, only after professional review, families who identify as neurodivergent.

For the first beta, either charge $19 to establish buyer behavior or provide access free in exchange for a scheduled interview and explicit feedback agreement. Do not trade a free product for a required positive review.

Measure:

- whether the guide was opened and the printable was used
- whether the family completed the seven-day experiment
- which boundary they chose
- whether the adult changed a behavior too
- how often device transitions became conflicts before and during the week, described as a practical observation rather than a clinical measure
- which replacement activities were actually chosen
- what felt unrealistic, shaming, confusing, or missing
- whether the parent would recommend the guide and how they would describe it in their own words

Use buyer language to refine the product title, sales page, age paths, and follow-up offer.

## Acquisition content

Create source-grounded articles and short videos that answer one real question each:

- How do I turn off the tablet without a meltdown?
- What should kids do instead of screens when I am exhausted?
- What screen rules should adults follow too?
- How do we handle school screens differently from entertainment?
- What changes for an autistic child or a child with ADHD?
- How do couples get on the same page about screens?
- What should happen to phones at dinner and bedtime?
- How do we recover after ignoring our own family rule?

Each piece should point to the free starter, not directly to the largest course.

## What not to build yet

- a screen-monitoring app
- a large parent membership
- a certification or facilitator license
- an AI parenting chatbot
- an expensive clinical-looking assessment
- a church curriculum before the general family practice works
- a universal claim that the same plan works for every child
